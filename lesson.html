02 / Learn the habits

Keep access.
Keep control.

Account security means keeping other people out of your accounts. Learn how to sign in safely, spot tricks, and ask for help.

5-minute reading time6-question checkNo account details needed

Today's learning goals

  1. Protect your sign-in.
  2. Check before you click.
  3. Get help if something goes wrong.

Use made-up examples in class. Do not change your school account settings unless your teacher says to.

01 / Strong sign-ins

Use a different password for each account.

If someone steals one password, they may try it on your other accounts. A different password for each account helps stop that trick.

  • Make passwords long. Aim for at least 16 letters, numbers, or symbols if the site allows it.
  • A password manager is an app that makes and stores passwords. Ask your teacher which one you may use.
  • A passphrase is a long password made of words. Use random words, not a name, birthday, or famous saying.
  • Do not send passwords in messages or share them with friends. Someone who claims to be tech support should not ask for your password.

Talk it through: Why is it risky to use the same password for a game and your email? Do not share a real password.

02 / Another layer

Add a second check when you sign in.

Multifactor authentication (MFA) adds another check along with your password. One example is a code from an app on your phone. This makes it harder for someone with your password to get in.

A passkey is another way to sign in. You unlock it with your device's number code (PIN), fingerprint, or face check. A passkey works with the real site, which helps protect you from fake sign-in pages. Ask an adult to help you set one up on a device you may use.

Keep sign-in codes private. Only enter a code when you started signing in on the real app or site. If a message asks you to approve a sign-in you did not start, choose “deny” and tell an adult. Do this even if the message keeps popping up.

03 / Stop and check

A message can look real and still be a trick.

Phishing (say “fishing”) is a scam. Someone pretends to be a person or company you trust. They want you to share secrets, click a link, or open a file. These tricks can come by email, text, chat, phone, or a QR code you scan.

Watch for surprise prizes, threats, and requests for passwords or codes. Good spelling and a logo you know do not prove a message is real. Even a friend's account can be stolen.

Look closely

The sender netfl1x-support@random-site.com uses a “1” instead of an “i.” The address also ends with a different site's name.

In roblox-security.fake-site.com, the word “roblox” is part of another site's address. It does not make this a Roblox site. Do not visit this example.

https:// means data is scrambled as it travels between you and the site. A scam site can use it too. It does not prove the site is safe.

What to do: Open the real app or use a saved web address you trust. Ask a teacher or your school's tech support team for help. They help fix computer and account problems. Report the message as your teacher shows you, then delete it.

04 / Daily habits

Keep your device safe, too.

  • Lock your screen when you step away. Keep your device and apps up to date.
  • Sign out when you finish on a shared computer. Just closing a tab may leave you signed in.
  • Do not save your passwords or set up personal passkeys on a shared school computer.
  • Ask an adult before you install an app or let an app use your account.

05 / Getting your account back

Made a mistake? Ask for help.

Tell a trusted adult right away. You do not have to fix this alone.

  1. Stop. Do not reply, share more information, or open files from the message.
  2. Ask for help. Tell your teacher, a parent, or school tech support what happened. Say if you shared a password, sent a code, or opened a file.
  3. Use the real app or site. With help, change any password you shared. Change it on other accounts where you used it, too. If you cannot sign in, use the real site's steps to get your account back.
  4. Check who has access. An adult can help you sign out other devices and remove unknown apps from your account. A new password may not sign out every device.

Plan ahead: Know how to get back into your account. Keep your recovery email up to date. A recovery email helps you reset a password. Store backup codes in a safe, private place. These codes can help you sign in if you lose your phone. Never send them to someone in a message.

Clicking a link does not always mean someone got into your account. Still, stop and tell an adult what happened.

Explore / Classroom tools

Put the lesson into practice.

  • Have I Been Pwned (opens in a new tab) — This tool checks if an email address was in a known data breach. A breach is when private data is stolen or shared without permission. Let your teacher show how it works. Keep real email addresses and results private. A “not found” result does not mean an account is fully safe.
  • PasswordMonster (opens in a new tab) — Test made-up passwords. See what happens when you add more letters or random words. The tool guesses how long a password might take to break. That guess is not a promise.

Use made-up passwords only. Do not test a real password, a changed version of it, or one you plan to use. These tools are on other websites. Their rules about saving or sharing data may be different.
