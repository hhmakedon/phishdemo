02 / Learn the habits

Keep access.
Keep control.

Account security means protecting how you sign in, noticing when something is wrong, and knowing how to get help.

Read · Discuss · Practice6-question checkNo account details needed

Today's learning goals

  1. Protect your sign-in.
  2. Verify before acting.
  3. Recover and report.

Practice with fictional examples only. Do not change school account settings during class unless your teacher directs you.

01 / Strong sign-ins

One account, one password.

Reusing a password means a leak at one service can put other accounts at risk. Use a different, long password for every account. Aim for at least 16 characters where supported.

  • Let a trusted password manager generate and store unique passwords. Follow your school's approved tools and rules.
  • If you need to memorize a password, use a long passphrase made from unrelated, randomly chosen words. Avoid names, birthdays, and common sayings.
  • Never share passwords in messages, with friends, or with someone claiming to be support.

Talk it through: Why could using the same password for a game and your email put both accounts at risk? Don't share any real passwords.

02 / Another layer

Use MFA. Consider a passkey.

Multifactor authentication (MFA) adds another kind of proof, such as a security key or a code from an authenticator app, alongside your password. Use it wherever it is available.

Passkeys let you sign in using your device's screen lock, such as a PIN, fingerprint, or face check. They are tied to the real service, which helps resist fake sign-in pages. Use them on personal or school-approved devices, and learn the recovery process.

Never send a one-time code to another person. Enter it only during a sign-in you started at the real service. Deny and report approval prompts you did not initiate. Repeated prompts are a reason to get help, not to tap “approve.”

03 / Pause and verify

A convincing message is not proof.

Phishing is an attempt to trick you into sharing information or taking an unsafe action by pretending to be someone you trust. It can arrive by email, text, chat, a QR code, or a phone call.

Watch for unexpected requests, rewards, pressure, attachments, and requests for secrets. Correct spelling, a familiar logo, or a calm tone cannot prove a message is genuine. Even a friend's account can be compromised.

Inspect the details

A sender such as netfl1x-support@random-site.com does not match the service it claims to represent.

In roblox-security.fake-site.com, the familiar brand is only part of a different site's address. Do not visit this example.

https:// means the connection is encrypted. A scam site can use it too.

Safer next step: Open the official app or a known bookmark to check the claim. Contact a teacher, school IT, or the organization through contact details you already trust. Report the message using the approved reporting tool, then delete it.

04 / Daily habits

Protect the device, too.

  • Lock your screen when you step away. Keep your device and apps updated.
  • On shared computers, sign out of accounts when finished. Closing a tab may leave you signed in.
  • Do not save passwords or create personal passkeys on a shared classroom device.
  • Install apps and extensions only from approved sources. Ask before granting account access to an unfamiliar app.

05 / Your recovery plan

If something happened, get help.

Mistakes happen. Reporting quickly helps protect you and others. You do not need to solve it alone.

  1. Stop interacting. Do not send more information, approve prompts, or open downloaded files. Tell a trusted adult or school IT promptly.
  2. Use the real service. From a trusted device, open its official app or known address. If you shared a password, change it there; change it anywhere else you reused it. If locked out, use official account recovery.
  3. Review access with help. Sign out other sessions, check recovery details and connected apps, and remove anything unfamiliar. A password change may not end every existing session.
  4. Strengthen recovery. Set up MFA or a passkey where allowed. Keep recovery contact details current and backup codes somewhere private and secure. Never share those codes in a message.

If you only clicked a link, that does not automatically mean your account was taken over. Describe what happened to the adult or IT staff helping you. If you downloaded or ran a file, tell them that too.

06 / Knowledge check

Choose the safer next step.

Answer all six questions, then check your reasoning. Your score stays on this page and is not sent to your teacher.

1. Why use a different password for every account?

2. A message asks for the sign-in code from your phone. What should you do?

3. A page uses https:// and looks professional. What does that tell you?

4. You receive an MFA approval prompt while you are not signing in. What next?

5. You shared a password on a suspicious page. What is the best response?

6. You finish using a shared classroom computer. What should you do?

Before you go

Your exit ticket

Tell a partner one habit you can use, one warning sign you would check, and one person you could ask for help. Keep personal account details private.
